How this course works

This course teaches interface design through rules you can apply the same day. Each lesson covers one rule, and every rule is shown on the same fictional page, before and after, so you can see exactly what changes.

You do not need to know how to draw, and you do not need to code. You need a design tool (the free plan of Figma is enough), a screen you want to improve, and about fifteen minutes per lesson.

  1. Read the rule and the reason behind it.
  2. Study the before and after figure.
  3. Do the short “Try it now” on your own screen.
  4. At the end of the chapter, do the exercise.

All interfaces shown in this course are fictional and were made for teaching.

Chapter 1

Color & type

Before a visitor reads a single word, they have already judged your interface by its colors and its letters. This chapter gives you five rules that make that first impression calm and clear.

Lesson 1.1

One accent color

Choose one color for actions, and use it nowhere else.

Why it matters

Color is a signal. When a button, a link, an icon and a heading each use a different bright color, nothing stands out and the eye does not know where to click.

A single accent color teaches the user one simple thing: this color means “you can act here”. The interface becomes easier to scan, and the main action becomes obvious.

How to apply it

  1. Pick one accent color that fits the brand.
  2. Use it for primary buttons, links and the active state of controls.
  3. Keep red and green for errors and success only.
  4. Remove color from anything that is not an action.

Mistakes to avoid

  • A different color for every button.
  • The accent color used as decoration, on large backgrounds or headings.
  • An accent so light that white text on it becomes hard to read.
Try it now

Count the bright colors on one of your screens. If you find more than one, decide which one means “action” and turn the others into neutrals.

Lesson 1.2

Neutrals for the rest

Backgrounds, text and borders live in white and grays.

Why it matters

Neutral surfaces let the content and the accent color breathe. Pastel sections compete with the content and make a page look like a patchwork.

A common guideline is 60–30–10: about 60% of the screen in a neutral background, 30% in secondary neutrals such as cards and text, and 10% in the accent color.

How to apply it

  1. Build a gray scale of five to nine steps, from almost white to almost black.
  2. Use the lightest step for the page background, and white or the next step for cards.
  3. Use the darkest steps for titles and body text.
  4. Use a light step for borders and dividers.

Mistakes to avoid

  • A different background color for every section.
  • Too many grays that look almost the same.
  • Tinted backgrounds behind long text.
Try it now

Take one colorful section of your page and rebuild it with only white, two grays and your accent color.

Lesson 1.3

Two fonts at most

One font family is often enough. Never use more than two.

Why it matters

Every typeface has a voice. Three voices on one screen is noise, and the interface looks unfinished.

Hierarchy should come from size and weight, not from a new font. A single family with several weights gives you everything you need.

How to apply it

  1. Choose one sans-serif family that has at least three weights.
  2. Use the weights (regular, semibold, bold) to create hierarchy.
  3. Add a second family only for a clear reason, such as code in a monospace font.
  4. For Arabic and Latin text together, choose two families with a similar weight and visual size.

Mistakes to avoid

  • A decorative font for body text.
  • A different font for buttons.
  • Bold created by the software instead of a real bold weight.
Try it now

List the fonts used on your screen. Replace all of them with one family and rebuild the hierarchy with weight only.

Lesson 1.4

A type scale

Use a few text sizes, with clear steps between them.

Why it matters

Random sizes such as 15, 17, 19 and 24 look almost the same, so the user cannot tell what is important.

A scale gives rhythm. When each size is clearly bigger than the previous one, titles, subtitles and body text are recognized at a glance.

How to apply it

  1. Start from a body size of 16 px.
  2. Choose a simple scale, for example 16, 24, 32 and 48.
  3. Limit yourself to four to six sizes and give each one a role: display, title, subtitle, body, caption.
  4. Set the line height to about 1.5 for body text and about 1.2 for large titles.

Mistakes to avoid

  • A title only two pixels bigger than the body text.
  • A new size invented for every screen.
  • Body text smaller than a card title placed next to it.
Try it now

Write down every text size on your screen. Reduce the list to five sizes and apply them.

Lesson 1.5

Enough contrast

Text must be readable by everyone, on every screen.

Why it matters

Light gray text looks elegant on a large monitor, but it disappears on a phone in daylight, and many people with low vision cannot read it at all.

The WCAG AA standard gives a clear target: a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text, and 3:1 for large text and for interface elements.

How to apply it

  1. Check every pair of text and background with a contrast checker.
  2. Use a dark gray on white for body text. In the figure, the pale gray reaches only 1.7:1, and the dark gray reaches 10.3:1.
  3. Check white text on your accent color too.
  4. Make sure placeholder and helper text can still be read.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Gray text on a gray background.
  • Text placed on a photo with no dark layer behind it.
  • Information given by color alone.
Try it now

Test the three lightest texts on your screen in a contrast checker. Darken every one that is under 4.5:1.

Chapter 1

Chapter summary

  1. One accent color, for actions only.
  2. White and grays for everything else.
  3. One font family, two at most.
  4. Four to six text sizes with clear steps.
  5. A contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for text.
Exercise: Redesign one screen
  1. Choose one screen from a project of yours, or redraw a simple landing page.
  2. Apply the five rules in order, one at a time.
  3. Keep a copy of the screen after each rule, so you end with six versions.
  4. Place the first and the last version side by side and note what changed.
